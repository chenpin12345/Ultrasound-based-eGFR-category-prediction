## Q1. Renal cysts: simple cyst criteria, complex cysts, Bosniak 2019, US/CEUS adaptations, parapelvic vs peripelvic vs hydronephrosis, PKD

### Takeaway
Bosniak v2019 is formally defined only for CT and MRI. Ultrasound adaptations exist mainly as the EFSUMB 2020 CEUS-adapted Bosniak, which tends to upgrade lesions because CEUS shows very thin septa and tiny nodules. Renal sinus cysts (parapelvic/peripelvic) are the classic "pseudohydronephrosis". The key discriminator is that the cysts do not communicate with or fill from the collecting system (excretory-phase CT).

### Cited Findings
**Bosniak v2019 (CT/MRI definitions)**
- Primary paper: Silverman SG et al., "Bosniak Classification of Cystic Renal Masses, Version 2019", Radiology 2019;292(2):475-488, doi:10.1148/radiol.2019182646 — via [Radiopaedia Bosniak v2019](https://radiopaedia.org/articles/bosniak-classification-of-cystic-renal-masses-version-2019)
- Class I: thin (≤2 mm), smooth wall; homogeneous simple fluid (−9 to 20 HU); no septa or calcification; the wall may enhance — [Radiopaedia Bosniak v2019](https://radiopaedia.org/articles/bosniak-classification-of-cystic-renal-masses-version-2019)
- Class II: thin smooth wall with 1–3 thin septa, hyperattenuating (≥70 HU) non-enhancing lesions, and similar; calcification of any type is allowed — [Radiopaedia Bosniak v2019](https://radiopaedia.org/articles/bosniak-classification-of-cystic-renal-masses-version-2019)
- Class IIF: minimally thickened (3 mm) smooth enhancing wall or septa, or ≥4 thin smooth enhancing septa → follow-up at 6 and 12 months, then yearly for 5 years — [Radiopaedia Bosniak v2019](https://radiopaedia.org/articles/bosniak-classification-of-cystic-renal-masses-version-2019)
- Class III: enhancing wall or septa that are thick (≥4 mm) or irregular (≤3 mm obtuse convex protrusions). Class IV: enhancing nodule ≥4 mm with obtuse margin, or any size with acute margin — [Radiopaedia Bosniak v2019](https://radiopaedia.org/articles/bosniak-classification-of-cystic-renal-masses-version-2019)
- Thickness vocabulary: thin ≤2 mm, minimally thick 3 mm, thick ≥4 mm. "Few" septa = 1–3 and "many" = ≥4. If the enhancing solid component is >25% of the volume, Bosniak does not apply; the lesion is a solid mass with cystic/necrotic change — [Radiopaedia Bosniak v2019](https://radiopaedia.org/articles/bosniak-classification-of-cystic-renal-masses-version-2019)
- The Radiopaedia v2019 article does **not** address US or CEUS. v2019 criteria are written for CT/MRI — [Radiopaedia Bosniak v2019](https://radiopaedia.org/articles/bosniak-classification-of-cystic-renal-masses-version-2019)

**US / CEUS adaptations**
- EFSUMB position statement: Cantisani V et al., "EFSUMB 2020 proposal for a contrast-enhanced ultrasound-adapted Bosniak cyst categorization", Ultraschall Med 2021;42:154–166, doi:10.1055/a-1300-1727 — cited in [Jin et al. 2024, Insights Imaging](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11607359/)
- The CEUS-Bosniak system has 5 categories (I, II, IIF, III, IV) based on echogenic content, calcification, septal enhancement, wall thickness, and protrusions. Applying the same criteria with CEUS tends to give **higher** categories than CT/MRI, because CEUS has higher contrast resolution and shows very thin septa and small protrusions. Upgrading is a particular concern in category III — [Jin DD et al. Insights Imaging 2024;15:285, doi:10.1186/s13244-024-01858-7](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11607359/)
- Jin 2024 results: compared with B-mode US Bosniak, CEUS more often down-classified lesions, which improved performance. AUC was 0.78–0.90 (experts 0.89–0.90, novices 0.78–0.80). Inter-rater agreement for category was AC1 0.75. Agreement on features ranged 0.44–0.94, with wall thickness and septa/wall quality the weakest. Nodule assessment explained 84% of category variability. Malignancy rates were IIF 6.9–14.3%, III 51.6–58.6%, IV 91.9–94.3% — [Jin 2024](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11607359/)
- An earlier Radiology study compared CEUS with CT for complex cystic masses. US classification had interobserver κ = 0.86, and CEUS and CT agreed completely on surgical vs nonsurgical cysts — [Radiology doi:10.1148/radiol.2431051924](https://pubs.rsna.org/doi/10.1148/radiol.2431051924) (year not confirmed from the snippet; likely 2007, vol 243)
- The EFSUMB CEUS categorization is intended to **complement** CT Bosniak, not replace it — [Thieme / EFSUMB-related record](https://www.thieme-connect.com/products/ejournals/html/10.1055/a-1503-4869) (secondary search snippet)
- CAR 2019 incidental renal mass recommendations ask for a Bosniak category on cystic lesions. They also want ultrasound to have a greater role than in earlier ACR guidance — [CAR 2019 PDF](https://car.ca/wp-content/uploads/2025/05/CAR-Recommendations-for-the-Management-of-the-Incidental-Renal-Mass-in-Adults_2019.pdf); ACR white paper (CT-centric) — [ACR Incidental Findings Committee renal mass white paper](https://wiki.radiology.wisc.edu/images/8/88/Management_of_the_Incidental_Renal_Masson_CT_A_White_Paper_of_the_ACRIncidental_Findings_Committee.pdf)

**Parapelvic vs peripelvic cysts (renal sinus cysts) vs hydronephrosis**
- Parapelvic cysts are true simple cysts with an epithelial lining. They arise from the parenchyma, protrude into the renal sinus, and are often solitary. Peripelvic cysts are not true cysts. They arise from sinus lymphatics, are typically multiple, bilateral, and irregular, and **encase** the pelvicalyceal system — [Radiopaedia parapelvic cyst](https://radiopaedia.org/articles/parapelvic-cyst)
- Renal sinus cysts can mimic hydronephrosis on US ("pseudohydronephrosis"). Unlike a dilated collecting system, the cysts **do not fill with contrast on excretory-phase CT**, though they may distort the pelvicalyceal system — [Radiopaedia parapelvic cyst](https://radiopaedia.org/articles/parapelvic-cyst)
- Key review with many figures: Rha SE et al., "The renal sinus: pathologic spectrum and multimodality imaging approach", RadioGraphics 2004;24 Suppl 1:S117–S131, doi:10.1148/rg.24si045503 — [Radiopaedia parapelvic cyst refs](https://radiopaedia.org/articles/parapelvic-cyst); also Nahm & Ritz, "The renal sinus cyst: the great imitator", NDT 2000, doi:10.1093/ndt/15.6.913
- Parapelvic cysts and extrarenal pelvis can be distinguished from true obstruction with a diuretic renogram — [Radiopaedia hydronephrosis](https://radiopaedia.org/articles/hydronephrosis)

**ADPKD**
- Pei Y et al., "Unified criteria for ultrasonographic diagnosis of ADPKD", JASN 2009;20:205–212, studied PKD1/PKD2 families of unknown genotype. Diagnosis: ≥3 cysts (unilateral or bilateral) at age 15–39; ≥2 cysts in each kidney at 40–59; ≥4 cysts in each kidney at ≥60. Exclusion: <2 cysts at ≥40 years — [AuntMinnie summary](https://www.auntminnie.com/clinical-news/ultrasound/article/15588532/researchers-propose-new-ultrasound-screening-criteria-for-adpkd); [Unimelb record](https://findanexpert.unimelb.edu.au/scholarlywork/303997-unified-criteria-for-ultrasonographic-diagnosis-of-adpkd)
- Press coverage says that in at-risk people aged 30–39, absence of any cyst makes ADPKD very unlikely. This was not verified in the abstract — [ScienceDaily 2008](https://www.sciencedaily.com/releases/2008/10/081022073717.htm)

### Inferences
- For a table, the "simple cyst" row is: anechoic, thin smooth wall, posterior acoustic enhancement, no internal Doppler flow (these classic grayscale criteria are not stated verbatim in the fetched sources; see Gaps). Confused with: a hypoechoic solid mass (RCC, lymphoma), hydronephrotic calyx, or renal pyramid. Distinguish by: through-transmission, absence of flow, no communication with the pelvis, and CEUS/CT if indeterminate.
- The B-mode US Bosniak and CEUS Bosniak categories are not interchangeable with CT Bosniak. Any label derived from US should record the modality.
- For ADPKD, cyst count per kidney is the US criterion, and its thresholds depend on age.

### Gaps
- I could not fetch a primary source stating the classic US simple-cyst criteria (anechoic, imperceptible wall, posterior enhancement, sharp back wall). The SRU 2024 consensus on incidental renal cystic masses ("Bosniak-like" US categories: simple, minimally complex, etc.) is something I recall existing, but this search could not find it. Verify via PubMed: "Society of Radiologists in Ultrasound consensus renal cystic masses 2024/2025".
- There is no formal "ultrasound Bosniak v2019" from the Bosniak working group. The 2019 paper lists US as outside its scope (this is my understanding, not verified in full text).
- ARPKD and pediatric "echogenic enlarged kidneys" appearance was not researched.

## Q2. Renal stones: features, twinkling, sensitivity limits, size overestimation, mimics (sinus fat, vascular calcification, nephrocalcinosis)

### Takeaway
A stone looks like an echogenic focus with a posterior acoustic shadow, with or without twinkling on color Doppler. However, grayscale US misses many small renal stones (sensitivity about 24–45% vs CT in older series) and overestimates size, especially stones ≤5 mm. Measuring the **shadow width** reduces the size error. Twinkling helps but its reported accuracy varies widely (55% to >90%).

### Cited Findings
**Detection sensitivity**
- Fowler KA et al., "US for detecting renal calculi with nonenhanced CT as a reference standard", Radiology 2002;222(1):109–113, doi:10.1148/radiol.2221010453. US found 24 of 101 CT-detected renal calculi: sensitivity 24%, specificity 90%. Per-stone accuracy was 54% and per-patient accuracy 77% — [RSNA](https://pubs.rsna.org/doi/10.1148/radiol.2221010453); figures via secondary summary [BestBETs](https://bestbets.org/bets/bet.php?id=964) (not verified against full text)
- Ray AA, Ghiculete D, Pace KT, Honey RJD, "Limitations to ultrasound in the detection and measurement of urinary tract calculi", Urology 2010;76:295–300. The literature review gave pooled US sensitivity of about 45% for renal and ureteric stones — [ResearchGate PDF](https://www.researchgate.net/profile/R-John-Honey/publication/41807605_Limitations_to_Ultrasound_in_the_Detection_and_Measurement_of_Urinary_Tract_Calculi/links/5d8a14a5a6fdcc8fd61b8cb8/Limitations-to-Ultrasound-in-the-Detection-and-Measurement-of-Urinary-Tract-Calculi.pdf); [EM-Consulte](https://em-consulte.com/article/445856)

**Size overestimation**
- Ray 2010: mean stone size was 7.4 mm on CT vs 9.2 mm on US (P = .018). For stones ≤5 mm, US overestimated by a mean of 1.9 ± 1.2 mm, and US and CT were discordant for 60% of stones ≤5 mm. Discordance was associated with skin-to-stone distance — [HealthDay](https://www.healthday.com/healthpro-news/conditions/ultrasound-may-often-overestimate-renal-stone-size-642197.html); [ResearchGate PDF](https://www.researchgate.net/profile/R-John-Honey/publication/41807605_Limitations_to_Ultrasound_in_the_Detection_and_Measurement_of_Urinary_Tract_Calculi/links/5d8a14a5a6fdcc8fd61b8cb8/Limitations-to-Ultrasound-in-the-Detection-and-Measurement-of-Urinary-Tract-Calculi.pdf)
- Dai et al. (30 clinical image sets): stone width overestimated by 3.8 ± 2.4 mm, but **shadow width** reduced the error to 1.0 ± 1.4 mm — [UroToday abstract](https://urotoday.com/recent-abstracts/endourology-urolithiasis/minimally-invasive-procedures/76558-tools-to-improve-the-accuracy-of-kidney-stone-sizing-with-ultrasound-abstract.html)
- Pediatric (Seattle Children's): width overestimated by 1.2–2.0 mm, while shadow width was closer to CT — [Acoustics.org, Lee FC](https://acoustics.org/evaluating-kidney-stone-size-in-children-using-the-posterior-acoustic-shadow-franklin-c-lee/)
- In vitro, overestimation increases with **gain and depth**. A grayscale-threshold method reduced the error, but no standard threshold could be set — [PMC5031098](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5031098); stone-mode US study [J Endourol doi:10.1089/end.2016.0341](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/abs/10.1089/end.2014.0332)

**Twinkling artifact**
- Definition: on color Doppler, a focus of rapidly alternating colors behind a strongly reflective, rough object (calculus), mimicking turbulent flow. The proposed mechanism is phase (clock) jitter in the Doppler electronics. It is more sensitive than acoustic shadowing for small stones, and most pronounced with a rough surface and the focal zone below the object — [Radiopaedia twinkling artifact](https://radiopaedia.org/articles/twinkling-artifact)
- Settings: a color scale of at least 60 cm/s is recommended to reduce intrarenal arterial aliasing, which can mimic twinkling (a false positive). Color gain affects its appearance. PRF effect is inconsistent — [Radiopaedia twinkling artifact](https://radiopaedia.org/articles/twinkling-artifact)
- Dillman JR et al., Radiology 2011;259(3):911–916, doi:10.1148/radiol.11102128: sensitivity for individual renal calculi was 55% and PPV 78%. Radiopaedia notes that about half of twinkling foci are false positive vs CT, making clinical value questionable — [RSNA](https://pubs.rsna.org/doi/10.1148/radiol.11102128); [Radiopaedia](https://radiopaedia.org/articles/twinkling-artifact)
- Other studies report much higher accuracy, mostly for **ureteral** stones. One series of 2,268 patients reported sensitivity about 97% and specificity about 90% vs CT — [World J Urol 2019 doi:10.1007/s00345-019-02773-z](https://link.springer.com/10.1007/s00345-019-02773-z). For renal stones <4 mm: specificity 100%, sensitivity 76.8% — [core.ac.uk record](https://core.ac.uk/works/99086279). **Conflict**: performance depends on location, size, machine, and settings, and I found no pooled meta-analysis.
- Other key refs: Lee JY et al. AJR 2001 doi:10.2214/ajr.176.6.1761441; Rahmouni A et al. Radiology 1996 doi:10.1148/radiology.199.1.8633158; Bacha R et al. J Med Ultrasound 2019 doi:10.4103/JMU.JMU_129_18 — [Radiopaedia twinkling refs](https://radiopaedia.org/articles/twinkling-artifact)

**Nephrocalcinosis (mimic and coexisting finding)**
- Nephrocalcinosis is calcium deposition in the renal **parenchyma**. About 95% is medullary, about 5% cortical, and combined forms are rare — [Radiopaedia nephrocalcinosis](https://radiopaedia.org/articles/nephrocalcinosis)
- Medullary nephrocalcinosis is about 20 times more common than cortical. It often coexists with nephrolithiasis, and medullary deposits can act as a nidus or migrate into the collecting system. Causes include hyperparathyroidism, medullary sponge kidney, distal (type 1) RTA, hypervitaminosis D, sarcoidosis, and furosemide (notably in neonates) — [Radiopaedia medullary nephrocalcinosis](https://radiopaedia.org/articles/medullary-nephrocalcinosis)
- US is superior to CT for medullary nephrocalcinosis in hypoparathyroidism: Boyce AM et al., JCEM 2013, doi:10.1210/jc.2012-2747 — [Radiopaedia medullary NC refs](https://radiopaedia.org/articles/medullary-nephrocalcinosis); review: Shavit L et al., "What is nephrocalcinosis?" Kidney Int 2015, doi:10.1038/ki.2015.76

### Inferences
- Stone vs sinus fat: both are echogenic. A stone usually has a **clean posterior shadow** and/or twinkling, while sinus fat is diffusely echogenic without a focal shadow (inference based on the twinkling and shadow literature above; there is no direct cited head-to-head study, see Gaps).
- Stone vs medullary nephrocalcinosis: nephrocalcinosis gives echogenic **pyramids** in a radial or bilateral distribution within the parenchyma. A stone sits in the collecting system or sinus. Shadowing may be absent in early nephrocalcinosis (my understanding; not verified in fetched text).
- For CS dataset labeling, stone size measured on US is biased upward. Do not use it as ground truth without CT.

### Gaps
- Fetched sources did not give text-level details on the US appearance of nephrocalcinosis (echogenic pyramids, grading 0–3, e.g., Dick/Hoyer grading) or the neonatal transient Tamm-Horsfall echogenic pyramids differential.
- I found no primary source on arterial (renal artery or arcuate) calcification as a stone mimic. Typically it is a linear or parallel "tram-track" echogenicity along the vessel, but this is unverified.
- Cortical nephrocalcinosis US pattern (e.g., chronic glomerulonephritis, cortical necrosis, oxalosis) was not fetched.

## Q3. Hydronephrosis: appearance, SFU, UTD 2014 (and 2021 update), adult grading, APRPD thresholds, mimics

### Takeaway
Pediatric hydronephrosis has two established systems: SFU (1993; grades 0–4) and UTD (2014 consensus; updated 2021), which uses APRPD plus calyces, parenchyma, ureter, and bladder. **Adults have no consensus grading**; "mild/moderate/severe" is subjective and has poor reliability. The main mimics are extrarenal pelvis, renal sinus cysts, and a full bladder, plus vessels (use color Doppler).

### Cited Findings
**General US appearance and adult grading**
- On US, hydronephrosis is a dilated pelvicalyceal system. Cortical thinning suggests chronic obstruction. US shows dilatation but not the cause — [Radiopaedia hydronephrosis](https://radiopaedia.org/articles/hydronephrosis)
- Adult grading as mild/moderate/severe is common, but **there is no preferred or consensus system** (as of about 2021). Grading systems have problematic intra- and inter-observer reliability — [Radiopaedia hydronephrosis](https://radiopaedia.org/articles/hydronephrosis); Onen A, "Grading of hydronephrosis: an ongoing challenge", Front Pediatr 2020;8:458, doi:10.3389/fped.2020.00458 — [PMC7481370](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC7481370/)
- A worldwide survey asked whether UTD has overtaken APD and SFU: Vallasciani S et al., Front Pediatr 2021, doi:10.3389/fped.2021.646517 — [Radiopaedia hydronephrosis refs](https://radiopaedia.org/articles/hydronephrosis)

**SFU (Society for Fetal Urology, 1993)**
- Grade 0: no dilatation, calyceal walls apposed. Grade 1: renal pelvis dilated only. Grade 2: pelvis plus a few calyces dilated, no parenchymal atrophy. Grade 3: all calyces dilated, with blunted fornices, flattened papillae, and possible mild cortical thinning. Grade 4: gross pelvicalyceal ballooning, loss of the pelvis–calyx border, and parenchymal thinning — [Radiopaedia SFU grading](https://radiopaedia.org/articles/sfu-grading-system-of-hydronephrosis); [Onen 2020](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC7481370/)
- Reliability: weighted κ was 0.223–0.400 for SFU grade 1 and 0.064–0.346 for grade 3 on prenatal US (Korean study). Another report gives κ 0.26–0.4 for grade 2 — [Yonsei record](https://ir.ymlib.yonsei.ac.kr/handle/22282913/87622) (via search snippet)
- The Onen system (a separate alternative) splits parenchymal loss at <50% vs >50% — [Onen 2020](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC7481370/)

**UTD classification**
- Original: Nguyen HT et al., "Multidisciplinary consensus on the classification of prenatal and postnatal urinary tract dilation (UTD classification system)", J Pediatr Urol 2014;10(6):982–998, doi:10.1016/j.jpurol.2014.10.002. Update: Nguyen HT et al., Pediatr Radiol 2022;52(4):740–751, doi:10.1007/s00247-021-05263-w — [Radiopaedia UTD](https://radiopaedia.org/articles/urinary-tract-dilation-classification)
- Antenatal A1: APRPD 4 to <7 mm at <28 weeks, or 7 to <10 mm at ≥28 weeks. A2–3: APRPD ≥7 mm (<28 weeks) or ≥10 mm (≥28 weeks), or any calyceal or ureteric dilatation, parenchymal abnormality, bladder abnormality, or unexplained oligohydramnios — [Radiopaedia UTD](https://radiopaedia.org/articles/urinary-tract-dilation-classification)
- Postnatal (>48 h of life): P1 = APRPD 10 to <15 mm or central calyceal dilatation. P2 = APRPD ≥15 mm, peripheral calyceal dilatation, or ureter ≥4 mm. P3 = P2 findings plus abnormal parenchymal thickness or echogenicity, or an abnormal bladder — [Radiopaedia UTD](https://radiopaedia.org/articles/urinary-tract-dilation-classification) (note: the Radiopaedia page describes the 2021 update wording; parenchymal thickness and echogenicity are graded only as normal or abnormal, with no numeric cut-off)
- "New language" review: Chow J et al., Pediatr Radiol 2017, doi:10.1007/s00247-017-3883-0 — [Radiopaedia UTD refs](https://radiopaedia.org/articles/urinary-tract-dilation-classification)
- Radiopaedia gives no formal SFU↔UTD mapping — [Radiopaedia UTD](https://radiopaedia.org/articles/urinary-tract-dilation-classification)

**Mimics**
- Extrarenal pelvis is a normal variant in about 10% of people. The pelvis lies outside the renal hilum and looks dilated on US, falsely suggesting obstruction. Calyces and cortical thickness are normal. CT, MRI, or diuretic scintigraphy resolves it — [Radiopaedia extrarenal pelvis](https://radiopaedia.org/articles/extrarenal-pelvis)
- Renal sinus (parapelvic/peripelvic) cysts cause pseudohydronephrosis. They do not opacify on excretory CT — [Radiopaedia parapelvic cyst](https://radiopaedia.org/articles/parapelvic-cyst)
- A full bladder or bladder outlet obstruction can cause bilateral pelvicalyceal prominence. **Rescan after voiding** — [Radiopaedia hydronephrosis](https://radiopaedia.org/articles/hydronephrosis)
- Radiopaedia's hydronephrosis article does not discuss vessels as mimics — [Radiopaedia hydronephrosis](https://radiopaedia.org/articles/hydronephrosis)

### Inferences
- Table logic: dilated **calyces** that connect to a dilated pelvis mean true hydronephrosis. An anechoic sinus structure that does not connect to the calyces and has rounded margins suggests a sinus cyst. A large pelvis outside the hilum with normal calyces suggests extrarenal pelvis. Anechoic tubular structures with color flow are hilar vessels. Dilatation that resolves after voiding is a bladder-distension effect.
- For an adult dataset label, record whether the grade is subjective (mild/moderate/severe). Expect label noise because κ is low.

### Gaps
- I did not fetch a primary source on prominent renal vein or artery mimicking a dilated pelvis (the color Doppler check is standard practice but uncited here).
- Adult APRPD thresholds have no consensus. I found no validated adult cut-off.
- The original 1993 SFU paper (Fernbach SK, Maizels M, Conway JJ, Pediatr Radiol 1993) was not fetched, so the definitions above come from secondary sources.

## Q4. Renal masses: AML vs RCC on US; when US is indeterminate

### Takeaway
A markedly hyperechoic mass as bright as sinus fat, with posterior shadowing, favors AML. A hypoechoic rim and intratumoral cysts favor RCC. There is significant overlap: small RCCs can be hyperechoic, so a hyperechoic mass on US usually needs CT or MRI (fat detection) or CEUS.

### Cited Findings
- Siegel CL et al., "Angiomyolipoma and renal cell carcinoma: US differentiation", Radiology 1996 (49 RCC, 35 AML). Shadowing occurred in 33% of AMLs and no RCCs. Hypoechoic rim and intratumoral cysts were seen only in RCC, though the numbers were small. AMLs were smaller and more often echogenic, but the overlap was significant — [WUSTL profile](https://profiles.wustl.edu/en/publications/angiomyolipoma-and-renal-cell-carcinoma-us-differentiation/); [QxMD](https://www.qxmd.com/r/8628873)
- Typical figure description: an AML is very hyperechoic, equal to renal sinus fat, with posterior shadowing. An RCC is hyperechoic but less than sinus fat, with a hypoechoic rim and internal cysts — [PMC5965995 review](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5965995)
- Fat-poor AML on CEUS: internal hyperechoic features (starry-sky and crescent signs) had AUC 0.88 in a single-center retrospective study of 154 tumors — [Front Oncol 2024 doi:10.3389/fonc.2024.1446801](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/oncology/articles/10.3389/fonc.2024.1446801/full)
- Pseudotumors: the most frequent are hypertrophied column of Bertin, persistent fetal lobation, and dromedary (splenic) hump. Contrast-enhanced power Doppler helps characterize them — Ascenti G et al., Eur Radiol 2001 (abstract via search); [Pitfalls in radiological evaluation of renal mass (secondary)](https://articl.net/diagnostic-radiology/pitfalls-in-radiological-evaluation-of-renal-mass)
- Column of Bertin features that favor a pseudotumor: isoechoic to cortex, continuous with cortex, smooth interface with the sinus, preserved renal contour, and homogeneous enhancement. Focal echogenic foci may represent interlobular vessels — [Cureus 2025 pediatric case, PMC12410592](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC12410592/)

### Inferences
- Table row "hyperechoic mass": confused with AML, RCC, column of Bertin (isoechoic, not hyperechoic), or sinus fat extending into a cortical scar. Distinguish with shadowing, rim, and cysts on US; then confirm fat on CT or MRI.
- For grayscale ROI analysis, an AML is a very bright outlier inside the cortex, similar to a stone but without a sharp shadow.

### Gaps
- Exact percentages of hyperechoic small RCCs were not retrieved. A commonly cited figure is that up to about 30% of small (<3 cm) RCCs are hyperechoic, but this is unverified here.
- I found no ACR Appropriateness statement on US-indeterminate solid masses. The CAR 2019 and ACR white paper (linked in Q1) cover management.

## Q5. Artifacts near the kidney and their effect on grayscale measurements

### Takeaway
The canonical review is Feldman MK, Katyal S, Blackwood MS, "US artifacts", RadioGraphics 2009;29(4):1179–1189 (doi:10.1148/rg.294085199; DOI from memory, citation confirmed via Radiopaedia). For the kidney, the key effects are: shadowing (stones, ribs, bowel gas), which darkens deeper tissue; posterior enhancement (behind cysts), which brightens deeper tissue; slice-thickness/partial volume, which fills cysts with fake echoes; mirror image across the diaphragm; reverberation; and side lobes producing pseudo-debris in cysts or the pelvis.

### Cited Findings
- Citation for Feldman 2009 RadioGraphics confirmed via the Radiopaedia reference list — [Radiopaedia reverberation artifact](https://radiopaedia.org/articles/31989)
- **Mirror image**: the beam reflects off a strong specular reflector (e.g., the diaphragm or lung interface). The system assumes a straight path, so it places a duplicate structure deeper, mirrored across the reflector — [Vegas 2021 physics of artifacts lecture](https://apil.ca/wp-content/uploads/2021/03/Vegas-2021-Physics-of-Ultrasound-Artifacts.pdf)
- **Reverberation**: the beam bounces between two strong reflectors and produces repeated, equally spaced echoes. Comet-tail is a related form; ring-down has a different mechanism — [Radiopaedia reverberation artifact](https://radiopaedia.org/articles/31989)
- **Side lobe**: false echoes from secondary lobes of beam energy place off-axis structures in the image, often as pseudo-debris inside anechoic cysts or a dilated pelvis — [Vegas 2021](https://apil.ca/wp-content/uploads/2021/03/Vegas-2021-Physics-of-Ultrasound-Artifacts.pdf); [articl.net US artifacts](https://articl.net/ultrasound/ultrasound-artifacts)
- **Slice thickness (partial volume, elevation-plane) artifact**: the beam averages tissues of different echogenicity across its thickness, so it **fills in hollow or cystic structures** with low-level echoes. It is reduced with a thinner elevation plane — [articl.net US artifacts](https://articl.net/ultrasound/ultrasound-artifacts); [Vegas 2021](https://apil.ca/wp-content/uploads/2021/03/Vegas-2021-Physics-of-Ultrasound-Artifacts.pdf)
- **Twinkling / color aliasing** (Doppler): covered in Q2 — [Radiopaedia twinkling](https://radiopaedia.org/articles/twinkling-artifact)
- **Gain and depth** inflate stone size, and gain is a main driver of measured brightness and size — [PMC5031098](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5031098)

### Inferences (grayscale bias, for the CS student)
- Acoustic shadow (stone, rib, bowel gas): a dark band below the reflector. An ROI that crosses the band has an artificially **low** mean gray value, which can be mistaken for hypoechoic cortex.
- Posterior acoustic enhancement (behind a cyst, or a full bladder for the lower pole): an artificially **high** mean below it. Cortex behind a cyst looks more echogenic, which could create a false "increased echogenicity / CKD" signal.
- Slice thickness and side lobe: cysts look less anechoic, so the mean inside a cyst rises and cyst masks based on intensity thresholds may under-segment.
- Refraction or edge shadowing at the curved renal poles or cyst edges produces thin dark lines that may be mistaken for cortical scars or interfaces.
- Mirror image across the diaphragm (right kidney/liver) can duplicate structures above the diaphragm. This is rarely inside a kidney ROI.
- Recommendation: place cortex ROIs away from shadows and enhancement columns, and record gain, depth, TGC, and probe frequency, because they shift absolute gray values.

### Gaps
- The Feldman 2009 full text was not fetched, so the DOI 10.1148/rg.294085199 is from memory and should be verified.
- Refraction (edge) shadowing and rib shadowing near the kidney: I found no kidney-specific primary source.
- I found no quantitative study measuring how much posterior enhancement or shadowing biases cortical mean gray value.

## Q6. Pitfalls: pediatric medullary pyramids, sinus fat vs stones, column of Bertin vs tumor, extrarenal pelvis

### Takeaway
Classic normal-variant mimics are prominent hypoechoic pyramids (especially in neonates and infants, where they can mimic cysts or dilated calyces), column of Bertin and dromedary hump (pseudotumor), extrarenal pelvis (pseudo-hydronephrosis), and echogenic sinus fat (which can hide or mimic stones). Each is resolved by continuity with normal structures, symmetry, and color Doppler, post-void scans, or other modalities.

### Cited Findings
- Column of Bertin, dromedary hump, and fetal lobation are the commonest pseudotumors. Discriminators are continuity with the cortex, isoechogenicity, homogeneous enhancement, and preserved contour — [PMC12410592](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC12410592/); [articl.net pseudotumors](https://articl.net/diagnostic-radiology/radiology-of-kidney-pseudotumors) (secondary); classic ref Yeh HC et al., Radiology 1992 (junctional parenchyma), listed in [Cambridge chapter refs](https://resolve.cambridge.org/core/books/pearls-and-pitfalls-in-abdominal-imaging/pseudotumor-due-to-focal-masslike-parenchyma/4A39F1CAB12C5BEE773116454130535F)
- Extrarenal pelvis: normal variant (~10%), mimics hydronephrosis, calyces normal — [Radiopaedia extrarenal pelvis](https://radiopaedia.org/articles/extrarenal-pelvis)
- Full bladder → bilateral pelvicalyceal prominence; rescan post-void — [Radiopaedia hydronephrosis](https://radiopaedia.org/articles/hydronephrosis)
- BMUS renal teaching resource (normal anatomy and variants) — [BMUS Renal PDF](https://bmus.org/static/uploads/resources/12.20_-_Renal_BMUS_JBURN_9.12.pdf)

### Inferences
- Pediatric pyramids: they are triangular, hypoechoic, arranged regularly around the sinus at the corticomedullary junction, with the base toward the cortex. Hydronephrotic calyces connect to the pelvis, and cysts are round and show posterior enhancement (based on standard teaching; no primary source fetched).
- For segmentation, pediatric pyramids should not be classified as cysts, and a cortex ROI should avoid pyramids (which have lower echogenicity) to avoid underestimating cortical brightness.

### Gaps
- I found no fetched primary citation for "prominent neonatal medullary pyramids mistaken for cysts/hydronephrosis". Suggested verification: Radiopaedia "renal pyramids" or pediatric renal US reviews (e.g., RadioGraphics pediatric renal US normal variants).
- Sinus fat vs stone: no primary comparative citation found (see Q2 Inferences).

## Q7. Effect of lesions on mean grayscale cortex analysis and ROI exclusion

### Takeaway
Published quantitative cortical-echogenicity / CKD studies handle confounders mainly by **excluding patients** with cysts, masses, hydronephrosis, or calculi, or by **masking** cysts and calyces out of the cortex ROI. No standard exists, and the quantitative tools are not yet validated for CKD grading.

### Cited Findings
- A CKD fibrosis US study excluded patients with multiple renal cysts, masses, hydronephrosis, or calculi "that could potentially impede image processing and analysis" — [Chen, Renal Fibrosis Assessment (PolyU)](https://ira.lib.polyu.edu.hk/bitstream/10397/116208/1/Chen_Renal_Fibrosis_Assessment.pdf)
- A Greater Mekong study masked renal cysts and calyceal areas in MATLAB so that only cortex was analyzed. Echogenicity was computed as pixel intensity and averaged across images — [GMSMJ, tci-thaijo](https://he02.tci-thaijo.org/index.php/gmsmj/article/download/262871/180904)
- A GLCM + ANN CKD classifier used three 50×50 ROIs (cortex, corticomedullary boundary, medulla) — [Diagnostics 2021, PMC8151922](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC8151922/)
- Yonsei used a deep learning pipeline: Mask R-CNN segmentation, then kidney length and kidney-to-liver echogenicity ratio, then a ResNet18 CKD classifier — [Yonsei record](https://ir.ymlib.yonsei.ac.kr/handle/22282913/193103)
- A veterinary study used mean gray value (MGV) for cortical echogenicity and noted that subjective echogenicity grading is unreliable — [J Vet Sci doi:10.4142/jvs.2020.21.e58](https://dx.doi.org/10.4142/jvs.2020.21.e58)
- A review cautions that these advances "have yet to be validated as quantitative tools" for CKD detection and grading — [PMC5640470](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5640470)

### Inferences
- Practical rule set for the project: (1) mask anechoic cysts, the hydronephrotic pelvis, and calyces (very dark, near 0); (2) mask echogenic foci with shadowing (stones, very bright) **and their shadow columns**; (3) mask the posterior-enhancement column behind cysts; (4) avoid pyramids and sinus fat; (5) consider normalizing to the liver or spleen (kidney-to-liver ratio) to reduce gain dependence. Steps 3–5 are inference, with no direct citation.
- Using a median or trimmed mean rather than a raw mean reduces sensitivity to small bright or dark outliers. This is a statistical inference, not literature-based.

### Gaps
- I found no paper that directly quantifies how much a cyst or stone inside an ROI changes cortical mean gray value.
